## [Claude uncovers origins of NIST ECDSA curve seeds](https://www.anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reports that Claude discovered the seeds behind the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 elliptic curves in under half an hour: none were derived from a hash algorithm as long assumed, but instead were outputs of the C library's lrand48 pseudo-random generator seeded with a Unix timestamp. Anthropic published the finding as a research post on ECDSA curve origins, with cryptography engineer Filippo Valsorda writing up the details. The seeds are the main public evidence that NIST's widely deployed curves were generated by a "nothing up my sleeve" process rather than deliberately chosen to embed a backdoor, so this finding partially settles a controversy cryptographers have argued about for decades and that intensified after the Dual_EC_DRBG scandal. It also serves as a striking demonstration of AI-assisted reverse engineering of long-standing open cryptographic problems. lrand48 is a 48-bit linear congruential generator from System V Unix, so its output is entirely determined by a single seed value; the discovery shows the seeds were simply timestamps fed to srand48, meaning they carry essentially no entropy and provide no cryptographic confidence in themselves. Crucially, the result explains how the seeds were produced but not how the curves were selected — any candidate curves generated with other timestamps and discarded are invisible, so a deliberate search over timestamps remains conceivable in principle, and the fifth binary seed B-163 still fits nothing Claude tried, with the simplest explanation being a typo introduced during publication.

hackernews · ahlCVA · Oct 16, 18:55 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50122800)

**Background**: ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm) is the signature scheme used in TLS/HTTPS, cryptocurrencies and countless security systems, and its security depends on the exact parameters of the named curves standardized by NIST. To prove those constants were not chosen to hide a weakness, standards bodies typically publish a "seed" — a value hashed to derive the curve parameters — so that anyone can reproduce the derivation and check that the designer had no freedom to pick a malicious curve. Because modern hash-based seeds look random but are verifiable, cryptographers long assumed the NIST seeds came from hashing some phrase; when that assumption failed to reproduce them, suspicion persisted for years, notably in the Koblitz–Menezes paper "A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma".

## [Mercor finds 90% of US work hours lack AI benchmark coverage](https://www.mercor.com/research/labor-index/benchmark-map/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Mercor published a "Benchmark Map" labor index finding that roughly 90% of American working hours are spent on tasks that existing AI benchmarks cover poorly or not at all, making it hard to judge how well models perform on the work Americans actually do. The analysis maps benchmark prompts against O*NET occupational task data and is described as a first step toward a continuously updated resource on AI capabilities across the economy. The finding suggests that headline benchmark scores may say little about real economic capability, potentially misleading researchers, policymakers and companies about which jobs AI can actually automate or augment. It also raises concerns about evaluation priorities, since the data indicates that tasks from high-wage occupations are overrepresented in benchmark prompts even after controlling for AI exposure. Coverage is judged at the task level using O*NET occupational definitions, and even a category like social workers counseling parents on child-rearing problems counts as "well covered" on the strength of just 13 prompts, a threshold commenters argue is far too loose to represent that work. The index is explicitly framed as a first step, so the methodology is expected to be refined and expanded over time.

**Background**: AI benchmarks are standardized test suites of prompts and tasks used to score model capabilities, but they are written by researchers and labs rather than sampled from the economy. O*NET, the Occupational Information Network maintained by the US Department of Labor, is a free database of around a thousand occupational definitions and the tasks, skills and abilities they require. Mercor's Benchmark Map joins the two to ask how much of the work in the US economy is actually represented by benchmark prompts.

## [Apple MLX Adds Distributed Inference, Runs 1T-Parameter Model on Four Mac Studios](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/mlx-distributed-inference) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Apple's machine learning research team published a new MLX distributed inference capability, released alongside a new version of MLX, that runs Meta's nearly trillion-parameter open-weight model Muse Spark Open across a cluster of four Mac Studios at over 36 tokens per second. A single Mac Studio with up to 512GB of unified memory can host the model first, with the setup then scaling out across multiple machines. It shows that frontier-class, near-trillion-parameter models no longer strictly require data-center GPUs, which is significant for privacy-sensitive, local and on-device LLM workflows and strengthens Apple silicon's position as a Mac-based inference platform. It also deepens the tie between Apple's MLX ecosystem and Meta's open-weight model releases, giving developers a practical alternative to H100-class hardware. The figures are measured on Muse Spark Open quantized to 4-bit, while Apple's single-Mac Studio comparison runs other models such as Qwen 3.8 27B and Mistral Medium 3.5 128B at native precision, so the throughput numbers are not apples-to-apples across model families. The distributed path relies on RDMA over Thunderbolt 5 connections between Macs, and scaling is limited by unified memory per node (up to 512GB) rather than by compute alone.

hackernews · antipaul · Oct 16, 13:43 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50118320)

**Background**: MLX is Apple's open-source array and machine learning framework built specifically for Apple silicon, taking advantage of the unified memory architecture so the CPU and GPU can operate on the same data without copying it between separate memory pools. Muse Spark Open, from Meta's Meta Superintelligence Labs, is a sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) open-weight model designed for long-horizon agentic coding work; MoE means only a subset of parameters is activated per token, which keeps inference cheaper than the total parameter count suggests. Distributed inference spreads a single model's weights and computation across multiple machines, and Apple's implementation uses tensor parallelism plus RDMA over Thunderbolt 5 to link Macs into a small cluster.

GPT-6 Astra leads the field at roughly a 57% score against human-written bot baselines, followed by GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Sonnet 5.5. ## [Rust framework DenseCore matches PyTorch bit-for-bit on nanoGPT CPU training](https://www.reddit.com/r/MachineLearning/comments/1x7ojxo/p_my_rust_framework_trains_nanogpt_with_the_exact/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A computer science student released DenseCore, a small Rust deep learning framework with a PyTorch-like Python API, claiming that pointing `import torch` at it and running Andrej Karpathy's nanoGPT unchanged produces bit-for-bit identical results to PyTorch on CPU — the same initial weights from `manual_seed`, the same loss, gradients, and weights across all 50 training steps tested, even with dropout enabled. The project ships as roughly 2 MB versus roughly 580 MB for PyTorch, and the author is publicly asking whether it is worth continuing. Bit-for-bit agreement between two independent implementations is a hard, rarely achieved bar, and the reverse-engineered PyTorch internals the author documents (flash-attention code paths, Adam's tail-loop rounding, `clip_grad_norm_`'s use of `reciprocal() * max_norm`) are directly useful to anyone chasing run-to-run reproducibility in deep learning. It also highlights the underappreciated fact that PyTorch itself is not reproducible across machines, which matters for CI pipelines, serverless inference, and debugging flaky training runs. The author found that PyTorch's CPU attention is not the textbook formula but a flash-attention kernel that switches to a different path when dropout is active, that Adam rounds differently on the last few elements of each thread's chunk because of compiler tail-loop fusion, and that `clip_grad_norm_` computes `reciprocal() * max_norm` instead of a division. A key caveat: PyTorch does not even match itself across machines — of 26 ops tested, only 1 produced identical bits between macOS and Linux x86 — so DenseCore only matches PyTorch on Apple Silicon Macs.

reddit · r/MachineLearning · /u/Mental-Feature-2377 · Oct 16, 18:49

**Background**: nanoGPT is Andrej Karpathy's minimalist, readable implementation of the GPT transformer architecture, widely used as a small-scale testbed for training code. PyTorch is the dominant deep learning framework, and FlashAttention is an IO-aware exact-attention algorithm that tiles the computation to reduce memory traffic, which is why its number sequence differs from a naive attention implementation. `clip_grad_norm_` is PyTorch's gradient-clipping utility that rescales gradients so their global norm stays below a threshold. Because floating-point addition is not associative, any change in the order or fusion of operations can change results, which is why matching another framework bit-for-bit requires replicating its exact kernel behavior.

## [StepFun Releases Step 5 Preview, a 600B Sparse MoE Agent Flagship](https://huggingface.co/stepfun-ai/Step-5-Preview) ⭐️ 7.0/10

StepFun released Step 5 Preview on Hugging Face, a flagship sparse Mixture-of-Experts model with 600 billion total parameters and 27 billion active parameters per token, supporting up to a 1 million token context window and accepting text, image, and video inputs. The model is positioned for agentic workflows and is reported to lead on software engineering, long-horizon agent execution, and specialized professional tasks, particularly in finance. The release puts StepFun in direct competition with other major Chinese labs shipping open-weight agentic models, and the combination of 600B total parameters with only 27B active keeps inference costs far below what a dense model of that size would require. Because it can be served through OpenAI-compatible APIs on vLLM or SGLang, enterprises and developers can self-host an agent-capable frontier model instead of relying solely on closed API providers. The model is distributed under the StepFun Model License 1.0, a custom license rather than a standard fully permissive open-source license, so commercial use terms should be reviewed carefully. It is also explicitly labeled a "Preview" release, meaning benchmarks and long-context behavior have not yet been independently validated by the community.

**Background**: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is an architecture in which a model contains many separate "expert" sub-networks but routes each token through only a few of them; a sparse MoE like this one therefore has a huge total parameter count while activating only a small fraction per token, trading memory for compute efficiency. A 1M-token context window lets the model process extremely long inputs such as entire codebases or long documents in a single pass, which is important for agent tasks that involve many steps. vLLM and SGLang are open-source high-throughput inference engines that implement OpenAI-compatible APIs, so a model served on them can be used as a drop-in replacement for proprietary APIs. "Agentic" models are tuned to plan, call tools, and execute multi-step tasks rather than just answer single questions.
