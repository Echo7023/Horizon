## [Levent Alpöge Claims Proof of Generalized Riemann Hypothesis with Huge Lean Formalization](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) ⭐️ 10.0/10

On November 20, 2026, mathematician Levent Alpöge posted a paper at alpo.ge/remark.pdf claiming a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, accompanied by an enormous Lean formalization distributed as a 1.3 GB archive of roughly 15 million lines split into 14 downloadable parts. The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis is one of the central open problems in number theory, and a valid proof would immediately settle the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions and turn hundreds of results currently conditional on GRH into theorems. The episode is also being watched as a landmark test of AI-assisted mathematics and of whether proof assistants can make claims of this scale checkable at all. The formalization dwarfs existing libraries such as Lean's mathlib by orders of magnitude, meaning that even machine-checkable code of this size poses a serious practical review and build-time challenge for the community. On the same day, Ravi Vakil posted a separate draft proof by Alessandro Masullo, made without AI assistance and under review by Brian Conrad, so there are now two competing claims to evaluate.

**Background**: The Riemann Hypothesis asserts that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function have real part 1/2; the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis extends the same assertion to the broader family of global L-functions, most famously the Dirichlet L-functions. Before this claim, the only proven analogues were in the function-field setting rather than for number fields, so the conjecture has remained entirely open in the classical case. Lean is a free, open-source proof assistant and functional programming language in which theorems are stated and checked by machine, most commonly on top of its mathematics library mathlib; a successful formalization would in principle let anyone verify a proof by running code rather than trusting referees.

Tristan Buckmaster published a GitHub repository containing a 79-page paper, dated 20 November 2026, titled "Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations." The paper claims to prove that solutions of the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time, and the claim was widely discussed on Hacker News as a major mathematical breakthrough. A correct finite-time blow-up result would resolve the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem — one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems — in the negative, overturning the long-held expectation that smooth 3D solutions persist for all time. It would also reshape how mathematicians and physicists think about turbulence and the limits of classical fluid models. The repository hosts the paper itself (paper/finite-time-blow-up-navier-stokes.pdf, 79 pages, version of 20 November 2026), and commenters stress that this is the unforced version of the problem, as opposed to results that break the equations using a carefully designed external forcing. **Background**: The Navier–Stokes equations are the standard partial differential equations describing the motion of fluids such as water and air. The Millennium Prize problem asks a deceptively simple question about them: given smooth initial data in three dimensions, do smooth solutions always exist for all time, or can they break down ("blow up") after a finite time? A blow-up proof answers that question by showing singularities do form, whereas a regularity proof would show they never do; either would earn the one-million-dollar Clay prize.

## [Thinking Machines Releases Inkling 2 Mini, an Open-Weights Looped Transformer](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weights model built on a looped-transformer architecture that was trained end-to-end in Nvidia's nvfp4 format. According to the release, the compact model reportedly rivals much larger models such as Qwen3-27B, and GGUF builds are already available so it can run locally on Ollama and llama.cpp. If the benchmarks hold up, this would be one of the strongest public demonstrations that architectural recursion can substitute for raw parameter count, potentially reshaping how open models are designed. Because the weights are openly available, it also puts a looped architecture into the hands of local-inference users and safety researchers long before comparable frontier designs are public. The team notes that looping improves the model's reasoning but does not let its weights store more facts — knowledge remains largely a function of total parameter count, so its AA-Omniscience factuality score is in line with other models of the same size. The nvfp4 training choice ties efficient low-precision numerics to Nvidia Blackwell hardware, and the model ships with both an "extra high" thinking mode and community GGUF quantizations from Unsloth.

hackernews · jasondavies · Nov 20, 20:12 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260)

**Background**: A looped transformer is a parameter-efficient, recursion-based variant of the standard deep transformer: instead of stacking many distinct layers, it repeatedly reuses the same block (or stack of blocks) across multiple passes, so effective depth grows without adding parameters. nvfp4 is Nvidia's 4-bit floating-point format introduced with the Blackwell GPU architecture, which groups only 16 FP4 values per block with an E4M3 scaling factor to keep accuracy higher than earlier 4-bit schemes. Inkling 2 Mini combines both ideas, aiming at strong reasoning in a model small enough for local deployment.

## [Frostbite: AI Discovers, Designs and Verifies a New CPU Architecture](https://architectlabs.com/blog/frostbite) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Architect Labs announced Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU architecture built around interaction nets that was discovered, designed, and formally verified end-to-end by the company's AI systems. The team claims the design reaches 4.59x the performance-per-watt of a modern superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU core. The claim suggests AI can compress the long, expensive R&D path from research-paper ideas to a verified hardware design, potentially reshaping how chip architects productize decades of academic work. If the results hold up, it would also give an entirely different computation model — interaction nets — a realistic path into mainstream hardware. The headline 4.59x figure is measured against an in-order, dual-issue baseline rather than a modern out-of-order core, and the benchmark suite is described by critics as small, so the performance claim remains unproven. The work also covers formal verification rather than a fabricated chip, meaning no silicon exists yet.

hackernews · shioyama · Nov 20, 10:01 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50572503)

**Background**: Interaction nets are a graph-based model of computation derived from the proof nets of linear logic: a program is a graph with agents at the nodes and wires connecting their ports, and computation happens when two agents' principal ports meet in an "active pair" and rewrite the graph locally. Because these rewrites are local and can in principle run in parallel, interaction nets have long been proposed as an alternative to the instruction-stream model of the 1940s von Neumann/Harvard architectures. Frostbite's novelty claim is that a general-purpose CPU can be built around this model, and that an AI pipeline — rather than human architects — can carry the idea from discovery through formal verification.
